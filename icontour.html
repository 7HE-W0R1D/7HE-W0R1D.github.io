iContour

A web platform where radiation-oncology residents practice outlining tumors and get instant feedback against an expert's contour.

At a Glance

Radiation oncologists must outline ("contour") tumors and organs on CT scans before treatment. Residents learn this through apprenticeship, which depends on busy faculty and gives slow, uneven feedback. At UC San Diego's Weibel Lab, I redesigned iContour's interface and rebuilt key parts of its backend, so residents can practice on real cases and immediately see how their contour compares with an expert's.

My Role

UI/UX designer and full-stack developer: designed and built the new web interface, maintained the Python backend on a Linux server, added data export and cleaning, and repaired the broken scoring analysis

Team

PI Nadir Weibel; PhD students Matin Yarmand and Chen Chen; undergraduate researchers including Kexin Cheng, Peter Liu and me; radiation oncologists Michael Sherer and James Murphy

Timeline

Jun 2022 – May 2024

Methods

Weekly design sessions with 5 clinicians over two semesters, usability testing, iterative redesign

Tools

HTML/CSS/JavaScript, Python, Jupyter, Ubuntu server

Outcome

Multi-institution pilot with 9 residents · CHI EA '23, DIS '24, IJROBP, ACRO '23

Designing with Clinicians

For two semesters, we met weekly with 5 radiation oncologists and residents. They contoured in the tool, told us what slowed them down, and tried the new version the week after. Pretty much every decision below came out of that loop.

Key Decisions

A dial for moving through slices

Before+1 / −1 buttons, slow when a scan has hundreds of slices. Clinicians asked for something faster.

ConsideredA horizontal timeline like a video editor

WhyA timeline would confuse anyone without video-editing experience. A "digital crown" dial scrubs quickly and still allows fine, single-slice moves.

Feedback on the same screen as the scan

ConsideredA pop-up or separate results page, which would give more room for information

WhyIn testing I noticed residents went back to compare their contour with the expert's after seeing their score; that comparison is where the learning happens. A separate page would make it hard or impossible, so the results panel sits beside the scan.

Show what the backend is doing

WhyScores take time to compute, and a blank wait made people unsure whether their submission had worked. The panel now shows calculation status before results appear.

Fixing the Engine

A nice interface doesn't help much if the numbers behind it are wrong. This was before AI coding tools, so I learned the lab's Python pipeline on a Linux server the slow way. I fixed the analysis function behind the scores (it had stopped working) and added data export and cleaning so the research team could actually analyze the study results.

Outcome

The team ran a pilot study with 9 residents from 5 institutions in 2 countries, published in IJROBP. My part was setting up the system for the study and collecting the data.

9 / 9named the visual comparison with expert contours the most useful feedback
4.67 / 5average usefulness rating
7 / 9found it more useful than their existing learning resources

The work also led to a DIS '24 paper on contouring across devices: in a lab study with 8 residents, contouring on a phone was about as accurate as on a desktop and significantly faster.

Reflection

What worked

Weekly contact with real clinicians. Small design changes were tested within days, and their feedback turned "a nicer UI" into decisions I could justify.

What I'd change

Much of my time went into backend plumbing. With today's AI-assisted development I'd spend that time gathering more feedback and iterating faster.

What I took away

In expert tools, the interface's job is to keep people close to the material. The best feedback sat right next to the scan.
