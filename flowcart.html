FlowCart

A modular robot cart that helps hotel housekeepers, and that greets, yields and says goodbye like good hotel staff.

At a Glance

Housekeepers push heavy, bulky carts all day, and they keep walking back and forth between the cart, the room and the laundry. With two teammates I designed FlowCart, a modular robotic cart, and built its first module: a laundry robot that "talks" to guests with hotel etiquette instead of a screen or a voice.

My Role

Prototyping lead: built the robot platform (hoverboard motors, ODrive, Raspberry Pi). I also co-ran interviews and user tests and co-wrote the paper.

Team

3 people: Nanyi Jiang (design and most user research), Xiaozhen Liu (user testing) and me

Timeline

Jan 2025 – Sep 2025

Methods

Shadowing, stakeholder interviews, product benchmarking, Wizard-of-Oz testing (studio and in the wild), expert interview

Tools

Raspberry Pi, ODrive motor controller, 3D modeling and printing

Outcome

Working laundry robot, tested twice · HRI '26 Companion paper

What We Saw in the Hotel

We shadowed housekeepers, housemen and managers at the Statler Hotel, a ~150-room hotel at Cornell, and interviewed its housekeeping supervisor, training manager and purchasing agent. Each housekeeper turns around 14 rooms a day at roughly 30 minutes per room.

  • Carts are heavy and clumsy. Workers back up and lean their body weight in just to get a cart moving, and the carts scrape hallway walls.
  • Constant back-and-forth. Staff walk between cart and room for supplies, and keep leaving the floor to take dirty linen to the laundry.
  • Hallways are guest-facing. Management is wary of anything that adds noise or flashing light on guest floors.
"[I need to] back up first to push the cart so it has the [momentum]." Housekeeper, during shadowing
"I wish there's a smaller [cart] that can bring things with us." Housekeeper, during shadowing

We also looked at what's already out there: cart pushers, auto-following trolleys, warehouse robots, and hotel service robots like Rosie and Whiz. They're good at hauling or cleaning. None of them really think about how a cart should act around guests in a hallway, so that's where we focused.

Key Decisions

Speak hotel etiquette, not robot

ConsideredVoice prompts or a screen

WhyManagement didn't want more sound or light on guest floors, and the hotel already trains staff in etiquette. A training manager taught us the "10-and-5 rule": within 10 feet of a guest, smile and make eye contact; within 5 feet, add a warm greeting. We translated four etiquette principles into lid and motion behaviors.

  • Proactive greeting: the lid lifts slightly to "notice" a guest, then wider to say "hi".
  • Clear communication: it slows down and pulls toward the wall to give way.
  • Gratitude: it turns after the guest and gives a small lid lift as a "goodbye".

A modular cart, starting with laundry

WhyReal carts are already organized in sections, so each section can become a module that moves on its own. We started with laundry because linen runs were the most frequent trips. An expert reviewer later pointed out that modularity also lets a hotel invest gradually.

A hoverboard drive on an off-the-shelf hamper

WhyWe needed enough torque for about 25 kg of laundry, cheaply and quickly. I followed Wendy Ju's lab's hoverboard-to-robot method: hoverboard wheels driven by an ODrive controller and a Raspberry Pi, mounted on a 100 L rolling hamper with custom 3D-printed brackets. TradeoffIt's fast and cheap but looks unfinished, which mattered more than we expected (see below).

The Hard Part: Housekeepers and Managers

For me the hardest part wasn't the motors. It was lining up my own ideas with what the hotel's managers were thinking. Housekeepers wanted something that takes the weight off and saves them trips. Managers were thinking about guests, cost, and what the hallway looks and sounds like. A lot of our decisions, like using etiquette instead of a screen and making the cart modular, came from trying to give both groups something they'd actually say yes to.

Testing and Iterating

Round 1: studio Wizard-of-Oz, 3 participants

Participants played hotel guests in a hallway while we drove the robot remotely. All three thought it was polite and wanted to know what was inside. But one wasn't sure if they were allowed to touch it, and another got confused by the hoverboard's colored wheel lights and tried to push the cart by hand. Backing up read as "giving way" much more clearly than just stopping.

Changes: we covered the wheel lights and I added a half-speed mode, so yielding could be a gradual, readable slowdown instead of an abrupt stop.

Round 2: in the wild, a university hallway

Nobody was briefed. 7 people walked past and the robot yielded to 5 of them. People often didn't notice it until it moved, and some got startled or hesitated. As a laundry hamper, it blended in a little too well.

Reflection

What worked

Building a working robot early let us test real behavior, not storyboards. Grounding every behavior in the hotel's own etiquette training gave us a design language staff and managers already understood.

What I'd change

I'd put more care into the finish. Participants reacted strongly to exposed parts and stray lights, and a rough look made the robot's intentions harder to read.

Open questions

How can the robot be noticed before it moves, without adding noise or light? How should it behave at the busiest times, and when and where should it charge? Next step: a deployment in the hotel itself.
