# Guidelines for an Effective UX Portfolio

*Source: INFO/COMM 3450 / INFO 5355, Week 13 Lecture — UX Communication*

## 1. Start with the rhetorical situation

A portfolio is a *genre* (a curated collection of work that shows your skills, process and impact). Its *medium* is a free-form website. Before you design it, answer these five questions:

| Element | For a portfolio |
|---|---|
| **Communicator** | You |
| **Purpose** | Catch the attention of recruiters and hiring managers. Show your **skills** and how you apply them, your **work** (process, decisions, outcomes), and your **personality** (language, look, style). |
| **Audience** | Main readers: employers and application reviewers. Others: professionals in other fields. |
| **Context** | The job market and other professional or academic openings |
| **Content** | About, Projects/Work, Resume, Contact |

## 2. Content: do

- A **1–2 sentence intro** about your passions, expertise and goals.
- A **longer bio / About** section for more background.
- Links to your **resume and LinkedIn**, kept up to date.
- A clear **way to contact you**.
- **High-quality images**.

## 3. Design: do

- Apply design principles to the **information architecture, user flow and navigation**.
- Make it **mobile responsive** and test it on several browsers and devices.
- Make it **accessible**: alt text, video captions, screen-reader support, color contrast, safe for color-blind viewers.
- **Check every link.**
- Pick a look and style that **represents you**.

## 4. Don't

- Pack any single page with text, images or animations.
- Use complex information architecture, flows or navigation.
- Use large media files that slow loading.
- Autoplay video or audio. Gentle animations are OK.
- Share sensitive personal information.

## 5. Case studies: tell the story

**Focus on process over outcome:** show *how and why* you designed, not just *what*. Follow this order:

1. The problem the project targeted
2. The context you worked in
3. The team, your role and your contributions
4. The activities: what you did, which tools, what came out of it. **Justify your decisions and tradeoffs.**
5. The challenges and how you handled them
6. The final outcome and, more importantly, **what you learned**

**Text: keep it economical**

- Short paragraphs and short sentences.
- Choose what to include and what to cut. Drop long details and long design descriptions.
- Reading time of **3–4 minutes or less**, and **no more than 3 scrolls**.

**Visuals: they should move the story forward**

- Use sketches, photos from research, prototype videos and similar material.
- Don't rely on visuals alone.
- Polish images so they look consistent.
- Avoid distracting animations and images that don't advance the story.
- **Caption every image and add alt text.**

## 6. What to highlight in each case study

1. **Real contact with actual users.**
   - ✗ "I designed an app for Cornell students."
   - ✓ "We ran 45-minute contextual interviews with 8 Cornell students and found a scheduling coordination problem."
2. **Collaboration plus your own contribution.** State the team size and a "My Contribution" line.
3. **Reasoning, reasoning, reasoning.**
   - ✗ "I can make beautiful Figma screens."
   - ✓ "I made these decisions, considered these alternatives and tradeoffs, and used this evidence."
4. **Reflection on failures and open issues**, for example a "what worked / what needed improvement" section.

## 7. AI is changing what reviewers expect

Hiring managers are growing **skeptical of portfolios that look too perfect**. They trust **evidence** that you can collaborate, contribute, reflect, reason, think divergently and convergently, take feedback and iterate. Replace or add to polished hi-fi screens with:

- **Photos:** fieldwork, affinity diagrams, usability test sessions, early sketches, hand-drawn storyboards.
- **Text:** research notes, interview excerpts, design decisions, reasoning, reflections.

## 8. Choosing projects

- **Junior designers should show 2–4 projects.** Pick the ones you're proudest of and can explain clearly.
- **Order them by strength, not by date.**
- Where projects come from: classes, internships, student organizations and clubs, hackathons, personal projects.

## 9. Treat the portfolio as a UX project

- Use your UX skills on it: user research, sketching and prototyping, user flows, heuristic evaluation, design reviews, usability testing with think-aloud.
- Look at many examples. Collect screenshots and links, and note what you like about each.
- Have fun with it and show yourself off.

## Self-check

1. Does it address the purpose, audience and context?
2. Does the short "about" sentence work?
3. Does the longer bio work?
4. Does it apply UX design principles?
5. Do the visuals support the content?
6. Is it accessible to users with disabilities?
7. What changes would make it more effective or easier to use?
