Designing playful robots to live with

Hi, I'm Borui. I explore the relationships people can have with robots when the robots aren't just serving us: when they're idle, curious, or busy with a task of their own. I research, sketch and build these experiences end to end, from fieldwork to working hardware.

Selected Work

Four projects I'd most like to talk about. Each one walks through the problem, who I learned from, the calls I made, and what I'd do differently next time.

Windborne

An installation that turns a wave of the hand into drifting light and soap bubbles, bringing a breath of wind into hospital waiting areas.

  • RoleDesigner & builder · 2-person team
  • MethodsSpeculative design, rapid prototyping, survey (n=30), in-person sessions
  • OutcomeDemo at ICMI '26 · 21 of 30 participants reported a mood lift

FlowCart

A modular robot cart for hotel housekeepers that greets, yields and says goodbye using hotel etiquette instead of screens.

  • RolePrototyping lead · 3-person team
  • MethodsShadowing & interviews at a 150-room hotel, Wizard-of-Oz testing
  • OutcomeWorking robot · HRI '26 Companion paper

iContour

A web platform where radiation-oncology residents practice outlining tumors and get instant feedback against an expert's contour.

  • RoleUI/UX designer & full-stack developer · UC San Diego
  • MethodsWeekly sessions with 5 clinicians for 2 semesters, iterative redesign
  • OutcomePilot with 9 residents at 5 institutions · CHI EA, DIS, IJROBP

UW Medicine

Redesigning how patients find care across 4 hospitals and 300+ clinics, for people of any medical or technical background.

  • RoleUX designer · 4-person capstone team · real client
  • MethodsField study, 6 think-aloud tests, affinity mapping, 2 prototype rounds
  • OutcomePlain-language search & location page template in Figma

About Me

When I graduated from kindergarten, everyone wrote their dream job in the class album. Teacher, scientist, astronaut. I didn't have a word for mine so I made one up: "Play-man", a person whose job is just to play. Honestly, I'm a bit surprised how close I've ended up.

Most afternoons after school I was in my dad's electronics lab, wiring LEDs to switches and sticking fans into plastic boxes just to see what would happen. In 7th grade I spent a whole weekend getting an Arduino to print ten characters, "Loading...", and I thought that was a great use of a weekend. I still do this. If the standard way is too easy, I'll happily spend three times the effort doing it my own way.

That's how I ended up in Human Centered Design & Engineering at UW, and then in Information Science at Cornell, a department that lets you wander around instead of sticking to one track. My research asks what machines would be like if they were allowed to play, and what kind of relationships people build with robots that are curious, idle, or just busy with their own thing.

How I work. I usually start from a feeling (wind lifting a dandelion, a heavy cart in a hotel hallway) and sketch interactions around it. Then I prototype fast, in pixels and in hardware, put it in front of people, watch what they actually do, and go again.

As a TA, students often tell me my explanations are easy to follow, mostly because I teach with lots of examples. Outside the lab I run a home server for my own AI, cloud drive and library, keep old iPods and phones alive, and fix my 2015 Volvo V60 myself. I usually end up spending more on tools than the repair would've cost.
